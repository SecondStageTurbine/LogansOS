The LogansOS Bible
A Foundational Charter for an AI-Native Operating System

Preamble
This document is the constitution of LogansOS â€” a clean-slate operating system descended in spirit from Altimit OS, written in Rust, targeting x86_64 (and later aarch64), with artificial intelligence and machine learning treated as first-class kernel concerns rather than userspace addenda.
This is not a Linux derivative. It is not a Unix clone. It borrows ideas where ideas are good, but it owes no compatibility debt to POSIX, to the Linux syscall ABI, to ELF dynamic linking conventions, or to the assumption that a CPU is the privileged compute substrate.
The bible is law for the project. When the bible and a pull request disagree, the pull request loses or the bible is amended â€” never both, never neither.

Part I: Philosophy
Article 1 â€” First Principles
1.1 LogansOS exists to answer one question: what does an operating system look like when intelligence is a kernel primitive, not an application?
1.2 Every design decision is evaluated against three axes, in this priority order:

Correctness â€” does it do what it claims, provably and without undefined behavior?
Coherence â€” does it fit the rest of the system, or is it a foreign organ?
Performance â€” is it fast enough for its role?

Performance is third, not first. A fast wrong answer is worse than a slow right one. A coherent slow system can be optimized; an incoherent fast one must be rewritten.
1.3 We build real operating systems engineering. Toy code, throwaway shortcuts, and "we'll fix it later" hacks are forbidden as a matter of project culture. Every commit is intended to ship.
1.4 We do not chase feature parity with Linux, Windows, or any other OS. Parity is not a goal; rightness is. If POSIX is wrong about something, LogansOS is allowed to disagree.
1.5 Usability is the purpose, not an afterthought. An operating system exists to get things done for a human being; it is not a project to be tended for its own sake. **LogansOS prioritizes usability as a first principle.** The north-star design test: **a five-year-old can pick up a LogansOS-powered laptop and intuitively know how to use it.** If a design fails that test, it is the design that is wrong, not the child.

- The mechanism is the AI-native premise itself (Articles 2 and 29): **Arthur -- natural language -- is the primary interface.** A five-year-old cannot navigate files, folders, applications, and settings menus, but they can speak and be understood. LogansOS is conversation-first / intent-first, not a desktop metaphor of nested menus that no child (and few adults) truly understand. A traditional GUI, where present, is a convenience layered on top, never the primary path.
- Usability is the END that Correctness serves, never its competitor. The 1.2 priority order (Correctness > Coherence > Performance) governs the *soundness of the substrate*; 1.5 governs what that soundness is *for*. Radical usability is **enabled by** the security floor, not traded against it: a child may act freely precisely because the capability system guarantees the machine cannot exceed what it was granted -- the yolo-within-guardrails posture (ADR-069): act smoothly, safe by construction. Any proposal that buys usability by weakening the correctness/security floor is a **false trade** and is rejected; yolo-within-guardrails is the standing proof that both are attainable at once.
- This is correctly a userland/experience concern (Article 6.6 bars policy and judgment from the kernel). The kernel's contribution to usability is the unbypassable CAN-not-exceed guarantee that makes a trusting, low-friction experience *safe*; the experience itself lives in Arthur and the userland.
- 1.5 is a **standing design lens**, not a slogan: every user-facing decision is evaluated against the five-year-old test, the way documentation is evaluated against the fresh-reader legibility test. When Arthur grows a real interface, this becomes a live design gate.

1.6 Beauty as craft. **LogansOS aspires to be beautiful — classic-Mac-grade craft is the bar** (PL, 2026-07-05). Beauty here is not decoration; it is 1.5's usability made visible, and it is governed by one operative lesson from the platform that set the bar and then lost it (the macOS Tahoe icon episode, 2026): **beauty comes from craft plus distinctness — enforced sameness is anti-beauty AND anti-usability.** When every icon is forced into the same rounded rectangle, things become harder to *recognize*; distinct silhouettes are how a human — and especially a five-year-old, who reads shape and color before words — tells things apart at a glance. Distinctness IS the five-year-old test applied visually.

- **Grammar, not uniformity.** A LogansOS design system supplies a consistent *grammar* — spacing, light, materials, motion — and must never erase distinct *identity*. Uniformity imposed for tidiness at the cost of recognition is a false trade, rejected the same way 1.5 rejects usability-vs-security trades.
- **Craft applies from the first pixel, now.** Today's visual surface is small (the boot frame, the framebuffer console, the shell) — the principle governs it anyway: every visual surface we ship is either crafted or an honestly-marked placeholder, never accidental (the OT-36 stale-boot-frame lesson: the boot screen is a self-report; keep it honest *and* make it worth looking at).
- **The GUI-phase specifics** (iconography, a full design language, visual identity) are a future phase's build (tracked in the ledger); the *principle* is standing from ratification, the same way 1.5 became a live lens before Arthur had a face.

1.7 The sovereignty moat. **LogansOS's durable value is owner-verifiable agency over local effects — the substrate, never the model.** An AI's raw capability arbitrages toward zero: whatever a model can *build* today, a model will simply *do* tomorrow. What does not arbitrage away is *where* it acts, *bounded by what*, *owned by whom*, and *reversible how*. The operative sentence: **any model can propose, but only the owner's substrate disposes** — the whole-system generalization of Article 29.3's "model proposes, capability disposes."

- **The moat is verifiable authority, not privacy marketing.** Incumbents own the words "privacy," "local-first," and "AI-native," and can ship excellent private-cloud AI — but their business models keep *platform root*. LogansOS's structural claim is that **the owner is the root of the stack**: no provider account, model gateway, policy update, or cloud-side state is required for core operation; authority is local and capability-bound; effects are reversible or explicitly confirmed; install/update is verified data, never privileged vendor execution; and the `neo>` shell remains an assistant-free owner path, so the assistant is always dismissable.
- **The brain is portable and always untrusted.** The principle survives only with brain-portability: the substrate binds *any* brain — the best local model the machine can run (the standing "best brain available" ruling), and, where the owner's policy permits, a remote frontier model as an untrusted planner through the same capability/reversibility boundary. A stronger model — local or remote — makes these rails *more* valuable, never obsolete: an unbounded powerful agent is more dangerous, so demand for a bounded, owner-rooted one grows with capability. Local-only purism is rejected; owner-rooted brain-portability is the posture.
- **Scope honesty.** This is a design/positioning first principle, not a commercial axiom: the monetization of sovereignty is unproven, and the claim holds for buyers who need *owner-verifiable* control of authority, state, rollback, and audit — not as a universal consumer-market claim. Kill conditions are banked in the ledger (OT-53) and this article is re-examined if one fires.
- *Provenance:* PL insight 2026-07-10 ("why have the model build it, if it can just do it?"), narrowed and hardened by a cross-model adversarial refutation (Codex-family, six attacks, verdict survives-but-weakened; `docs/THINK-2026-07-10-ot53-sovereignty-moat-refutation.md`). Only the narrowed, refutation-surviving form is constitutional.
Article 2 â€” The AI-Native Premise
2.1 AI/ML is not a userspace library that the kernel happens to host. The kernel is aware of inference workloads and treats tensors, models, and inference contexts as scheduling and memory citizens.
2.2 Concretely, this means:

The kernel exposes a tensor object as a kernel-managed primitive, alongside processes, threads, and memory mappings.
The scheduler distinguishes inference threads from compute threads and from I/O threads, and applies different policies.
The memory manager understands model residency â€” large weight tensors are mapped, pinned, and shared across processes via copy-on-reference (not copy-on-write) semantics.
Accelerators (GPUs, NPUs, TPUs when present) are kernel-managed devices with first-class drivers, not userspace-hijacked PCIe BARs.

2.3 We reject the CUDA/ROCm/Metal model where userspace processes own the accelerator. The kernel arbitrates accelerator access the same way it arbitrates CPU time and physical memory.
2.4 We reject the "AI is a service over a socket" model (LM Studio, Ollama, llama.cpp daemons). Inference is a syscall family, not an IPC dance.
2.5 This does not mean the kernel runs neural networks. It means the kernel schedules and protects them. The actual matmul happens in driver code, accelerator firmware, or a dedicated kernel inference module â€” but the kernel knows it is happening and to whom.
Article 3 â€” What We Are Not
LogansOS is not:

A Linux distribution, fork, or rebadge.
A research microkernel demonstration. We are building a usable system, not a paper.
A *surveillance-driven* "AI assistant OS" in the consumer-product sense (a cloud chatbot persona plus data harvesting). LogansOS *does* ship an integral assistant — Arthur (Article 29) — but on-device, capability-bounded, and telemetry-free (Articles 23.1â€“23.2): the opposite of the consumer data-harvesting model. *(This bullet was narrowed 2026-06-14 when Article 23.1 was reversed: the original "no built-in chatbot persona" became "no surveillance persona.")*
A unikernel or single-application appliance.
A cloud-only OS. We target bare metal first; virtualization is a guest scenario, not a host one.
A POSIX-compatible system natively. The native ABI is our own. Foreign-OS compatibility, including POSIX/Linux, is provided through opt-in personality subsystems per Article 5.6 â€” not by adopting POSIX as the native shape, and not by extending the native ABI to mimic POSIX semantics piecemeal.


Part II: Architectural Pillars
Article 4 â€” Kernel Architecture
4.1 LogansOS is a monolithic kernel with disciplined module boundaries, not a microkernel. We chose this because:

Microkernel IPC overhead is hostile to inference workloads where data is large and frequent.
Driver-as-userspace is a Linux-era assumption; the cost/benefit collapses when most "drivers" are accelerator firmware blobs anyway.
Monolithic does not mean unstructured. Internal module boundaries are enforced by Rust's module system and crate boundaries.

4.2 The kernel is written in Rust, #![no_std], with unsafe permitted only at hardware boundaries (asm, MMIO, page tables, MSRs) and reviewed line-by-line. C is not used in the kernel proper. Assembly is used only where Rust cannot express the operation (boot trampolines, context switches, syscall entry, ring transitions).
4.3 No external kernel-level dependencies beyond what is required for bringup: x86_64, bitflags, spin, linked_list_allocator, and similarly minimal crates. Every dependency is a liability. Every dependency is justified in writing in Cargo.toml comments.
4.4 The bootloader is Limine, not GRUB, not a custom one. Limine gives us a clean modern boot protocol with HHDM, framebuffer, modules, and SMP support without legacy BIOS baggage.

4.5 LogansOS is single-node by design. The kernel runs on one physical machine. Cross-node persistent state, single-system-image clustering, distributed shared memory across hosts, and kernel-resident cluster-membership protocols are out of scope and will not be added by amendment without superseding this clause. The kernel exposes network primitives (Article 11) on which applications and middleware build distributed systems; the kernel itself does not span machines. This matches the canonical division between substrate and application: LogansOS is the substrate, distributed systems run on top.

Article 5 â€” Userspace Architecture
5.1 The native binary format is ELF64, but the dynamic loader is ours and the ABI is ours. We do not promise GNU/glibc compatibility.
5.2 The native syscall mechanism is SYSCALL/SYSRET on x86_64, with our own syscall numbering. Linux numbering is not preserved.
5.3 The standard userspace runtime library is libneo, written in Rust, #![no_std]. libneo wraps syscalls, provides a minimal allocator, and exposes the AI/ML kernel primitives to applications.
5.4 The first userspace process is neo-init (PID 1). It is the supervisor; it spawns and reaps all other processes. It is intentionally minimal â€” service management is its own subsystem, not init's job.
5.5 Userspace is multi-process from Phase 1.5 onward. Threads within a process share an address space; processes do not. There is no fork() â€” process creation is spawn() with an explicit ELF or memory image, by design. Fork is a 1970s artifact that fights every memory-management technique invented since.
5.6 LogansOS natively supports its own ABI. Foreign-OS binary compatibility, when provided, is delivered through opt-in personality subsystems that translate foreign syscalls to native kernel operations at the syscall boundary. Personality layers are kernel-resident, opt-in per process (bound at exec time and inherited by descendants), and isolated from the native ABI by construction: a personality-tagged thread cannot invoke a native syscall by number, and a native thread cannot invoke a personality syscall by number.

Personality layers must not alter native ABI semantics. Native applications never observe the presence or absence of any personality. The native ABI evolves on its own schedule and does not negotiate with personality requirements.

Personality subsystems live under kernel/src/personality/<name>/. Each personality is justified by its own ADR; the existence of one personality does not authorize others. Personalities are statically compiled (Bible Article 9.2 forbids loadable kernel modules) and may be conditionally compiled out via Cargo features for embedded or minimal builds.

5.7 Compatibility boundary rule. Foreign-OS compatibility belongs at the personality boundary, not in the native core. The scheduler, memory manager, VFS, network stack, graphics/accelerator substrate, and capability/security model remain LogansOS-shaped. Personality-specific behavior lives in `kernel/src/personality/<name>/` or in personality-owned userland services. Shared core hooks are allowed only when they are general LogansOS primitives useful outside a personality; they require ADR-level justification and must not become scattered `if Linux` / `if Windows` branches. Multiple personalities may run concurrently on the same kernel, but each process has one personality at a time, and no personality may pollute native ABI semantics.
Article 6 â€” The AI/ML Subsystem
6.1 The AI subsystem lives in kernel/src/ai/ and exposes a syscall family in the 0x100..0x17F range (reserved). The AI block was originally reserved as 0x100..0x1FF; its upper half 0x180..0x1FF is owned by the network subsystem (Phase 4 native ABI, syscalls 0x180..0x18E), so the AI reservation was narrowed to the still-free lower half 0x100..0x17F to resolve the overlap (amendment 2026-05-31). It is structured in three layers:

Layer 0 â€” Tensor primitive. The kernel-managed tensor object: shape, dtype, device residency (CPU RAM / accelerator VRAM / shared), refcount, lifetime tied to a process or made global.
Layer 1 â€” Model object. A loaded model is a graph of tensor operations plus immutable weights. Models are kernel objects, refcounted, shareable across processes.
Layer 2 â€” Inference context. A running inference is a scheduled entity â€” like a thread but with explicit accelerator residency, deadlines, and priority class.

6.2 Models are loaded from a native model format (.neom), not ONNX or GGUF directly. Importers from ONNX/GGUF/SafeTensors live in userspace tools that produce .neom for the kernel to load. The kernel reads exactly one format. Userspace tools convert.
6.3 The kernel does not include a particular neural network architecture's logic. Transformers, CNNs, diffusion models â€” these are all expressible in the tensor-op graph the kernel understands. The kernel cares about ops and tensors; semantics are the model file's business.
6.4 Accelerators are exposed via the accel driver class. A driver registers its capabilities (supported ops, dtypes, memory size, dispatch latency). The kernel's inference scheduler chooses where to run each op based on the model's preferences, the accelerator's load, and policy.
6.5 When no accelerator is present, the kernel falls back to a CPU inference backend. CPU inference is correct and slow; this is acceptable. We do not ship a system that requires a GPU to boot.

6.6 The kernel exposes inference-governance hooks but does not implement governance policy. Specifically, the AI subsystem provides capability-gated mechanisms â€” LogitPort being the canonical example (ANSPEC-002 Â§7) â€” by which a holding process can observe, veto, or reweight the logit distribution of an inference context before sampling. The mechanism is the kernel's contribution; the policy is the application's. The kernel does not ship a default safety filter, alignment layer, or content classifier. It ships the primitives by which userspace builds those things, capability-gated like every other kernel object.

This clause exists because the alternative â€” a kernel-resident governance policy that runs on every inference by default â€” is the failure mode Article 23.3 (and the kernel/userland boundary) already forecloses, in a less-obvious form. A "default safety filter the kernel applies on every inference" is a *kernel-resident* AI persona by another name and an embedded specific model by another name; both are forbidden in the kernel. 6.6 is the positive version of that prohibition: the kernel exposes the seam on which governance can be built; the kernel does not stand on that seam itself. *(Note: the 2026-06-14 reversal of 23.1 permits an integral assistant — Arthur — but only in **userland**; this clause's prohibition on a kernel-resident persona/model is unchanged and is exactly what keeps Arthur out of the kernel.)*

Article 7 â€” Memory Model
7.1 Virtual memory is mandatory. Real-mode and unpaged execution exist only during the boot transition.
7.2 The kernel occupies the upper half of the 48-bit canonical address space (0xFFFF_8000_0000_0000 and above). Userspace occupies the lower half. The split is enforced by the page tables, not by convention.
7.3 Each user process has its own PML4. Kernel-half PML4 entries are shared by entry-copy on process creation. Cross-process page-table updates to the kernel half are propagated; user-half updates are private.
7.4 Physical memory is managed by a frame allocator. The bootstrap allocator is a bump allocator (Phase 1); a buddy allocator replaces it (Phase 2); a slab allocator sits atop the buddy for object pools (Phase 2). No kmalloc-style heap fragmentation games.
7.5 Tensor memory is a distinct allocation class, managed by the AI subsystem with awareness of accelerator residency. A 70B-parameter model's weight buffer is not a normal kernel allocation and is not subject to the slab allocator.
7.6 Demand paging exists. Swap does not, by default â€” modern systems have enough RAM, and swap is an attack surface and a latency hazard. A future phase may add swap as an opt-in.
Article 8 â€” Concurrency and Scheduling
8.1 The scheduler is a multi-class priority scheduler with the following classes, in priority order:

Real-time (RT) â€” bounded-latency threads, e.g., audio, input handling.
Inference â€” AI workloads with explicit deadline hints.
Interactive â€” UI and short-lived foreground threads.
Compute â€” CPU-bound batch work.
Background â€” opportunistic, yields readily.

8.2 Inference workloads have their own class because their scheduling is fundamentally different: they are bursty, often have soft deadlines (token-per-second targets), and frequently block on accelerator dispatch rather than CPU.
8.3 SMP is supported from Phase 2. Per-CPU state is real (per-CPU GDT/TSS/IST, per-CPU run queues, per-CPU current-thread). Global locks are forbidden in hot paths; we use per-CPU structures and lock-free or fine-grained locking.
8.4 Threads are kernel-managed. There is no green-thread / M:N / fiber abstraction at the kernel level. Userspace may build coroutines on top, but the kernel sees OS threads.
8.5 Critical-section discipline. The kernel honors a written discipline for code that holds locks and that runs in interrupt context. The following rules apply kernel-wide.

8.5.1 Spinlock and interrupt discipline. Any spinlock that may be acquired from both interrupt context and process context must have IRQ-safe acquisition: the process-context acquirer disables local interrupts before taking the lock and restores the prior interrupt state on release. The kernel provides IRQ-safe lock primitives for this purpose. Plain spinlocks are reserved for locks that are exclusively process-context (no ISR ever takes them) or exclusively interrupt-context (no process ever takes them). The choice is documented at lock declaration via the type used.

8.5.2 No heap allocation in interrupt context. Interrupt handlers and their transitive callees may not allocate from or deallocate to the global heap. This includes timer handlers, IPI handlers, exception handlers, and any function reachable from them. ISR-context code paths use pre-allocated, fixed-size, or stack-bounded data structures. The global heap may not be made structurally safe to use from ISR context as an excuse to violate this rule; the rule stands regardless of allocator implementation.

8.5.3 Scheduler heap-independence. The scheduler's critical sections â€” any code path that holds a per-CPU scheduler lock â€” must not depend on the global heap allocator's availability. Scheduler-internal data structures use bounded or pre-allocated storage so that scheduling operations never trigger heap allocation. The scheduler's correctness is independent of whether the heap is currently locked, fragmented, exhausted, or otherwise unavailable.

8.5.4 No blocking, yielding, or being preempted while holding a spinlock.

A spinlock may not be held across any operation that can block, sleep, yield, or wait on a condition. Operations that acquire other spinlocks are permitted only with documented lock-order discipline; operations that acquire mutexes or semaphores from spinlock-held context are forbidden. Code that needs to perform blocking operations must release all spinlocks first.

Additionally â€” and this is the discipline that SC-3B's Hpw mechanism (commit e2b3f4f, 2026-05-10) was caused by violating: a spinlock acquired in preemptible context, where the same lock may also be acquired in IF=0 (interrupts-disabled) context on the same CPU, MUST be acquired with local interrupts disabled by the preemptible-context acquirer. The kernel-provided `without_interrupts(...)` wrapper is the canonical pattern; type-level enforcement is the subject of ADR-019 (see `docs/ADR-019-switchmutex-irq-off-by-construction.md`).

Failing this discipline produces the **Hpw failure mode**: a preemptible-context holder is preempted by an interrupt; the same CPU then enters an IF=0 acquirer that spins on the lock; the preempted holder cannot resume because the IF=0 spinner monopolizes the CPU; the system is in a single-CPU self-deadlock that an SMP scheduler cannot route around. See `Plans/CLOSE-REPORT-2026-05-10-sc-3b.md` Â§3 and Â§4 for the canonical analysis and the discipline's first instance.

8.5.5 Enforcement. These rules are enforced by code review at PR time, by unsafe-block SAFETY comments per Article 15.2 where unsafe code is involved, and structurally by the type system where lock primitives encode the discipline. Future tooling (lockdep-equivalent runtime checks, static analysis) is anticipated but not required to ratify any individual rule.

Article 9 â€” Drivers and Hardware Abstraction
9.1 Drivers live in kernel/src/drivers/ and follow a uniform trait-based interface per device class (block, net, console, accel, input).
9.2 Driver loading is static at compile time for Phase 1â€“3. Loadable kernel modules are explicitly deferred and may never arrive â€” we would rather ship a clean monolith than reinvent the Linux kernel module disaster.
9.3 PCI/PCIe enumeration is done by the kernel; ACPI parsing for newer hardware. UEFI is the firmware target; legacy BIOS is unsupported.
9.4 USB, SATA, NVMe, and modern networking (e1000e, virtio-net, virtio-blk for VMs) are the priority device classes. Specific phase-binding for each device class is enumerated in 9.5â€“9.10. Legacy hardware (IDE, ISA, BIOS-era pre-USB peripherals) is not a target; the only legacy exceptions are the boot-path graceful-degradation cases noted explicitly in the relevant sub-clauses.

9.5 Architecture coverage, with phase-binding:

x86_64 â€” primary target, required from Phase 0, all current development.
aarch64 â€” secondary target, Phase 5 or later, after the AI subsystem foundation lands. The foundation work is architecture-portable by design; bringup requires real boot validation on at least one ARM target.
RISC-V (riscv64) â€” not in current plan. Adding RISC-V requires an ADR with contributor commitment.

Architectures beyond these three are out of scope until proposed via ADR.

Modern x86_64 laptops with UEFI firmware (Intel and AMD) â€” primary target. Driver coverage prioritizes what's needed to boot and run on this class.
Modern x86_64 desktops with UEFI firmware (Intel and AMD) â€” same priority as laptops; driver overlap is large.
x86_64 servers with UEFI â€” stretch target. Server-specific concerns (BMC, IPMI, advanced storage controllers) are not blockers.

Embedded / SoC â€” out of scope without ADR.

USB gamepads, joysticks, drawing tablets â€” Phase 8+ (userland phase). Not a kernel-blocker.
Bluetooth input â€” Phase 8+ at earliest. Requires a Bluetooth stack which isn't in any current phase plan; ADR if pursued.

AI accelerators â€” their own driver class per Article 6.4 and ANSPEC-001; not graphics.

Article 10 â€” Filesystem and Storage
10.1 The native filesystem is NeoFS (designed in Phase 3). Until then, tmpfs and a read-only Limine-module filesystem suffice.
10.2 NeoFS is content-addressed at the block layer, with a POSIX-ish path namespace on top. Models, when stored, are deduplicated by content hash â€” loading the same 70B model twice from disk costs one copy in cache.
10.3 Files have typed extensions at the FS level â€” not in the filename, in the inode. A .neom file is tagged as a model; the kernel can refuse to mmap it as executable code. This kills entire classes of confusion attacks.
10.4 We will ship a FAT32 and ext4 read-only driver for interop. We will not ship write support for foreign filesystems unless absolutely necessary.
Article 11 â€” Networking
11.1 Deferred to Phase 4. The Phase 1â€“3 kernel has no network stack.
11.2 When implemented, the stack is in-kernel (smoltcp-derived or original), supports IPv4 and IPv6 with IPv6 as the default, and exposes a socket-like API that is not BSD sockets. We will design our own and document why.
11.3 TLS is a kernel concern at least to the extent that the inference subsystem may need authenticated model downloads. The crypto code is audited and minimal; we do not roll our own primitives, but we do roll our own glue.
Article 12 â€” Security Model
12.1 Capability-based security from Phase 2. Every kernel object (file, tensor, model, process, thread, mapping) is referenced by a capability handle. Holding the handle is permission; there is no ambient authority.
12.2 No root user. Privilege is per-capability, not per-uid.
12.3 SMEP, SMAP, NX, KASLR, and W^X are mandatory from Phase 1. UMIP, CET shadow stacks, and LAM are enabled where the CPU supports them.
12.4 The kernel never executes user-provided code in kernel mode. Period. There is no eBPF equivalent in Phase 1â€“3. If we ever add programmable kernel hooks, they are bytecode-verified offline and signed.
12.5 AI models are a security boundary. A loaded model can read its inputs and write its outputs and nothing else. Model-injection-via-weights attacks are a real threat; the kernel treats model weights as data, never as code.

Part III: Engineering Discipline
Article 13 â€” The Phase Model
Development proceeds in numbered phases. Each phase has a goal milestone, an explicit scope, and explicit anti-scope. A phase is complete when:

All criteria pass.
No anti-criteria triggered.
Real boot verification on QEMU and on at least one bare-metal target.
A single squashed commit lands on main with the phase tag.

Current phase ledger:

Phase 0 â€” Boot, framebuffer, serial. Complete.
Phase 1.1 â€” GDT, IDT, basic interrupts. Complete.

Article 15 â€” Code Standards
15.1 Rust 2024 edition. No nightly features beyond what #![no_std] kernel work absolutely requires (currently: naked_functions, abi_x86_interrupt, pointer_is_aligned_to, and a small named set documented in kernel/src/lib.rs).
15.2 unsafe blocks are accompanied by a // SAFETY: comment explaining the invariant being upheld. No exceptions. CI fails if an unsafe block lacks a SAFETY comment.
15.3 No unwrap() or expect() in kernel paths that can be reached after kmain returns from init. Init code may panic on failure (we cannot recover from a broken GDT). Steady-state code must handle errors.
15.4 No global mutable state without a lock or a documented single-writer invariant. static mut is permitted only with a written justification in a comment naming the invariant and the migration target (per-CPU, atomic, lock-protected).
15.5 Formatting is rustfmt with our rustfmt.toml. Linting is clippy with warnings-as-errors. Both run in CI.
15.6 Every public kernel function has a doc comment. Every module has a //! summary explaining its role.
15.7 Lean patch rule. Every byte must buy correctness, observability, or a required capability. Code paths, abstractions, dependencies, configuration switches, and test scaffolding that do not earn one of those roles are bloat and must be removed before merge. Simpler fixes are preferred when they preserve the same invariant and verification strength. Machinery added for convenience, speculative future-proofing, aesthetic symmetry, or "maybe later" surface area is rejected. Project shorthand: if a change would not survive tight-machine scrutiny, it is not a patch; it is hexadecimal landfill. This is a review heuristic, not a new hardware target and not permission to sacrifice correctness.

Part IV: AI/ML Specific Doctrine
Article 19 â€” Why AI in the Kernel
The conventional argument against kernel-resident AI is that ML changes too fast for a kernel. We disagree, and here's the precise argument:
19.1 What changes fast in ML is model architectures. Transformers in 2017, MoE in 2023, state-space models in 2024, whatever in 2026. Architectures are graphs of tensor ops.
19.2 What changes slowly is the set of tensor ops. Matmul, softmax, layernorm, attention, convolution, elementwise â€” this set has been stable for a decade and grows by accretion, not replacement.
19.3 What changes very slowly is the resource-management problem: scheduling compute on heterogeneous accelerators, managing large persistent memory regions, isolating tenants, accounting for use.
The kernel handles (19.3). The kernel exposes ops from (19.2) as primitives. Architectures from (19.1) are user-defined graphs. This factoring is stable.
Article 20 â€” The Tensor Object
20.1 A tensor is (shape, dtype, device, storage). Shape is a small fixed-rank descriptor (rank â‰¤ 8). Dtype is one of an enumerated set (initially: f32, f16, bf16, i32, i8, u8, bool â€” extensible). Device is a kernel-tracked accelerator id or cpu. Storage is a refcounted physical-memory or accelerator-memory region.
20.2 Tensors are reference-counted kernel objects. A handle to a tensor is a capability. Sharing a tensor across processes shares the storage; the kernel tracks per-process handles.
20.3 Tensors are immutable by default. Mutable tensors exist (for activations during inference) but are explicitly marked. Weight tensors are immutable. This makes deduplication and safe sharing trivial.
Article 21 â€” The Model Object
21.1 A model is (graph, weights, metadata). The graph is a DAG of tensor ops referencing weight tensors and input tensors and producing output tensors. The graph is loaded from a .neom file and validated by the kernel.
21.2 The kernel validates models on load: shape consistency, dtype compatibility, op support on at least one available device, no cycles. A model that does not validate does not load.
21.3 Multiple processes may share a loaded model. The weights are mapped read-only into each process's tensor namespace. Inference produces per-call output tensors owned by the calling process.
Article 22 â€” The Inference Context
22.1 An inference call is a syscall: inf_run(model_handle, inputs, outputs, flags) -> result. The kernel schedules the model's ops onto available accelerators, manages intermediate storage, and returns when the output tensors are populated.
22.2 Inference is interruptible at op boundaries. A high-priority inference can preempt a low-priority one. The kernel's inference scheduler enforces this.
22.3 Inference is accountable. Each call records compute used, memory peak, and accelerator time. This is exposed to userspace for cost accounting and to the scheduler for fairness.
Article 23 â€” What We Will Not Do
23.1 The OS ships an integral, on-device assistant — **Arthur** (Assistant for Real-time Tasks, Home automation, & User Requests; see Article 29) — its native interface for natural-language command and capability-bounded autonomous action. Arthur is a first-class, defining component of the OS (realized in userland on the model-agnostic kernel substrate per 23.3; on-device with no telemetry per 23.2), not a surveillance persona and not a cloud service. Applications may also build their own assistants on top. *(This article was reversed 2026-06-14, by PL decision under Articles 24.1/27, from its original prohibition — "We will not ship a system AI assistant; the OS does not have a chatbot persona." The prohibition guarded against a surveillance chatbot gimmick; the substrate built since — capability-bounded authority, kernel-governed residency, on-device inference — makes an integral assistant defensible the way the original article presumed impossible. The kernel-level guarantees that backed the prohibition are preserved verbatim in 23.3 and the Article 6.6 boundary: the kernel remains model-agnostic and runs no resident persona — Arthur lives in userland. See Article 29 and the footer amendment note.)*
23.2 We will not collect telemetry, training data, or usage data from the OS. Inference happens on the user's hardware on the user's data and stays there.
23.3 We will not embed a specific model in the kernel. The kernel is model-agnostic.
23.4 We will not block on the AI/ML community settling on a "right" inference framework. We define our own; importers handle compatibility.

Part VI: Product Principles
Article 28 â€” Product Accessibility
28.1 LogansOS is built to be usable by real people on hardware they already own. This is a product principle, not a marketing slogan; it constrains scope decisions throughout the project.

28.2 "Usable by real people" means, concretely:

Installable on a common modern laptop or desktop (per Article 9.6) without requiring exotic hardware, custom firmware, or developer-grade test rigs.
Boot, log in, type, point. A user with a fresh LogansOS install on supported hardware can power on, see a working display, use their keyboard and mouse or touchpad, and reach a working userspace shell or graphical environment, depending on phase. This is the floor.
Read existing files. LogansOS can read the FAT32 and ext4 partitions a user already has (Article 10.4), so transitioning is not "wipe everything and start over."
Run useful software. Foreign-OS compatibility is delivered through opt-in personality subsystems (Articles 3, 5.6). The Linux personality (ADR-023) is the first such subsystem; once delivered in Phase 8+, common Linux software runs on LogansOS hardware with no per-application porting. LogansOS is not Linux, but it is not so alien that the existing software ecosystem is unreachable.

28.3 Accessibility is bounded, not infinite. LogansOS does not aim to be Linux. It does not aim for hardware coverage parity with any existing OS â€” Article 1.4 still applies. The accessibility goal is satisfied when a competent user with supported hardware can install LogansOS and accomplish meaningful work without expert-level system administration knowledge.

28.4 Decisions about scope expansion (new device class, new architecture, new userspace feature) are evaluated against accessibility: does this expansion materially help a real user reach the bar in 28.2? An expansion that helps is candidate scope; one that doesn't is deferred or rejected. This test is applied alongside Articles 1.2 (correctness > coherence > performance) and 1.4 (no parity-chasing); it does not override them.

28.5 Accessibility is a goal, not a delivery date. Phases 0â€“4 build the substrate that makes accessibility possible; Phases 5â€“7 deliver the AI-native value proposition; Phase 7.5 lands WiFi support per ADR-017; Phase 8+ delivers the userland that brings the accessibility goal within reach of non-developers. LogansOS at Phase 2 (current planning horizon) is not yet accessible to a non-developer; the bible commits to making it so over the project's lifetime.

Article 29 â€” Arthur, the Integral Assistant
29.1 LogansOS ships with an integral assistant named **Arthur** â€” Assistant for Real-time Tasks, Home automation, & User Requests. Arthur is a first-class, defining component of the OS, in the sense that folders are integral to a desktop OS: not an optional application a user installs, but part of what LogansOS *is*. Its character is friendly, practical, and oriented to everyday life â€” a tool for getting things done, not a personality for its own sake.

29.2 Arthur does two things, and they are the state of the art an operating system should embody: (a) **natural-language command** â€” the user tells the machine what to do in plain language and it does it; and (b) **capability-bounded autonomous action** â€” Arthur may take initiative and make its own decisions on the user's behalf. Both are first-class, intended capabilities of the OS, not bolted-on conveniences.

29.3 The safety keystone is structural, not aspirational. Arthur's authority is **bounded by the kernel's capability system by construction** â€” it can reach only what it has been granted, enforced by the same mechanism that governs every other principal. This makes an autonomous assistant on LogansOS *structurally safer* than a userspace agent with ambient authority on a conventional OS: "intelligence as a kernel primitive" (Article 2) extends to "the assistant's reach is kernel-governed." Autonomy is acceptable precisely because authority is bounded.

29.4 Arthur is realized in **userland on the model-agnostic kernel substrate**, and this boundary is load-bearing, not incidental. The kernel governs, isolates, accounts, and serves the models Arthur uses, but **embeds none** (Article 23.3); Arthur is not a specific model but the assistant *layer*, and the model it runs is swappable and hot-swappable (ADR-049). Arthur runs **on-device** on the user's own hardware, on the user's data, with **no telemetry, ever** (Article 23.2). This is the deliberate inverse of the cloud-chatbot-plus-data-harvesting consumer model the project still rejects (Article 3): an integral assistant built the right way â€” governed, bounded, private, local.

29.5 Arthur is the committed telos of the accessibility goal (Article 28): a conversational, capable assistant is the most concrete form of "usable by real people on hardware they own." The substrate (Phases 5â€“7.5) is the proven foundation; Phase 8 (inference maturity + serving + userland) and the agent layer that follows build Arthur up on it. The realization roadmap is recorded in docs/VISION-logansos-to-jarvis.md (the project's committed direction; "JARVIS" there is the historical working placeholder for what is now named Arthur). Specific build deadlines remain honestly phase-gated â€” Arthur is the committed *direction*, proven-vs-pending stated plainly per Article 1, never an over-claim.
