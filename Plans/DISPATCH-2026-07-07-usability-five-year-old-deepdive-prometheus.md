## The thesis to sharpen (frame the research)
Article 1.5's mechanism is the AI-native premise: **Arthur / natural language is the primary interface** — a five-year-old can't navigate files/folders/menus but can speak and be understood; the desktop metaphor is a convenience layered on top, never the primary path. This dive makes that concrete: *what specifically makes a conversation-first OS intuitive-by-construction, better than Windows on the axis that matters (a non-expert getting real work done without a manual)?* Ground every principle in real HCI/usability prior art, not first principles.

## What to determine (real source — HCI literature, usability research, the actual voice-assistant post-mortems; cite the real work, never a summarizing fetch)
- **Q1 — Intuitive-by-construction: the HCI foundation.** The proven principles: Norman's gulf of execution/evaluation, discoverability, affordances, feedback, recoverability, recognition-over-recall. For a *conversation-first* system, which of these transfer, which change, and which become *easier* (e.g. recall→recognition collapses when you just ask instead of hunting a menu)? Cite Norman/Nielsen/the usability canon. The output: the handful of principles a LogansOS interaction must satisfy.
- **Q2 — Why Windows (and the desktop metaphor) fails the five-year-old — the anti-patterns to structurally avoid.** Concrete, sourced: the install ritual (run-a-stranger-as-admin, Next/Next/Accept), nested mystery menus, unattributed errors ("something went wrong, code 0x80070005"), modal-dialog blindness, feature-discovery-by-archaeology. For each, name *why* it's hard for a non-expert and how conversation-first + our architecture (OT-44 install-is-data; Arthur explains errors in plain language; no menus to discover — you ask) structurally removes it. The PL has *lived* several this session (the BSOD, the install-as-execution horror) — use them.
- **Q3 — Voice/conversational-UI prior art: what works and why the assistants plateaued.** The honest post-mortem of Alexa/Siri/Google Assistant: they were conversation-first and still didn't replace the desktop — WHY? (Discoverability of what-you-can-say, the "did it hear me" feedback gap, no recoverability, no real agency/tool-use, cloud-dependence.) What must Arthur do differently — and note where our design already answers it (OT-26 real agency + tool-use; OT-19 reversibility = recoverability; the persona = feedback; on-device = trust). This is the sharpest lesson: we're not the first conversation-first OS attempt; learn from why they stalled.
- **Q4 — The clarify/feedback loop as the usability mechanism (ties to the reliability dive + OT-46).** How intent-based systems handle "I didn't understand" gracefully — the clarify-don't-guess loop (OT-26 D4) is not just a reliability fallback, it's the *usability* recovery mechanism (a five-year-old says the wrong thing; the system asks a simple question, doesn't error). Design the interaction pattern: how Arthur surfaces "I can do A or B, which?" in the OT-46 butler register (`VISION-arthur-persona.md` example 6) so failure is *conversational recovery*, not a dead end.
- **Q5 — The accessibility-generalization (the curb-cut effect).** Designing for the five-year-old is designing for everyone — the non-technical adult, the elderly user, the stressed expert. State how the five-year-old bar, met, produces a system *more* usable for capable users too (not a dumbed-down one). Cite the curb-cut/universal-design literature. Guards against the failure mode where "simple" means "limited."
